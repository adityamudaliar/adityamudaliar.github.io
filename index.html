B2B2C native & web products

Utec by UltraTech is a home-building companion for people building their own house in India, and a business channel for the architects, engineers and contractors who help them. This is how we designed it, from the first field visit to tested prototypes.

Client
UltraTech Cement, Aditya Birla Group
Products
Android and iOS apps for house builders and for partners, plus a responsive web app
Scale
More than 1,000 screens
My role
Led UX: research, workshops, information architecture, flows and usability testing

The problem

Building a house takes months. Nobody stays with the owner from the first plan to moving in.

An individual house builder has to find land, get approvals, raise a loan, hire contractors, buy materials and supervise labour, mostly on word of mouth. Every stage brings new people to trust and new ways to lose money. UltraTech asked a simple question: could it become the one trusted partner across that whole journey?

Most of these builders live in rural areas and Tier 2 to Tier 4 towns. Many use an Android phone on mobile data, mainly for WhatsApp, Facebook and banking, and type in their own regional script. The product had to work for them first.

The project covered house builders and partners. This case study follows the house builder side.

Goals

  • Guide house builders from end to end, so they don’t have to piece the journey together alone.
  • Give them enough knowledge to make their own decisions, instead of depending on middlemen.
  • Make the experience good enough that people want to come back to it.
  • For UltraTech: lifelong brand association, higher recall, and new routes to cross-sell, wallet share and market share.

Approach

We followed the double diamond. First we went wide in research and narrowed down to insights and “how might we” questions. Then we went wide again in ideation, and built, tested and iterated before anything reached development.

Before going into the field, we sketched a hypothetical journey with five phases: need, planning, construction, interiors and moving in. It gave the research a structure: where are the triggers, the pain points and the moments of truth?

Research across India

100house builders at different stages of construction
11regions, from Ludhiana to Thirumangalam
27–55+age range of men and women we met

We met first-time and repeat builders, nuclear and joint families, and people who hand everything to a contractor as well as people who manage every labourer themselves. We spoke to the main decision maker and to the family members who shape each decision: spouses, fathers, sons and brothers.

Each visit became a profile. Ramana Reddy, a 42-year-old shop owner in rural Telangana, starts work at 5am, seven days a week. He owns a city plot he bought long ago and wants to build there before he retires. He uses his phone only when he’s free, on mobile data, in his own language. Designing for him meant designing for limited time, moderate confidence with technology and a strong preference for his own language.

Making sense of it

In design thinking workshops with stakeholders, we turned field notes into frameworks. A Jobs To Be Done canvas showed what people were really trying to do. The practical jobs were to stay on budget and on time, get approvals and find an architect they could trust. The internal jobs were to feel in control and get through it with less stress. Deeper still, they wanted to provide for their family and build a legal house without being cheated on materials.

We then mapped products and services against those jobs. Gain creators included proactive estimates, help visualising the design and help with finance and approvals. Pain relievers included advice at every step, certified contacts rated by other builders, and tools to track cost and time.

The journey map brought it together. For the planning, construction and interior phases, it lists tasks, feelings, thoughts and opportunities. Feelings moved from confused to cautious to quietly happy. The opportunities row became the product backlog: a land rate predictor, approval guidelines, expense and milestone trackers, 3D and VR previews, and a directory of certified contractors.

Sketching solutions

We sketched quickly in the workshops, one idea per sheet, so the team could react to ideas instead of arguing in the abstract.

Structure

The information architecture grouped more than a dozen areas, from onboarding, search and notifications to financial help, permissions and learning, so each could grow without crowding the home screen.

Before drawing screens, we mapped task flows for the riskiest journeys: visualisation, onboarding, the estimator and the project tracker.

Wireframes

Onboarding asks about the house, but people can skip it with “Do it later” and come back through a persistent card. A checklist of what they’ve already done replaces a long questionnaire and shapes the home feed. The menu sits at the bottom right, within reach of a thumb.

Expert and material search lists architects, engineers and contractors on a map and as cards, with filters for distance, opening hours and ratings, and profiles that show real projects.

The cost and time estimator gives a quick standard estimate, or a detailed one when you upload a 2D layout. It also points you to the nearest UltraTech Building Solutions (UBS) store.

Testing

We tested clickable prototypes with house builders, watching what they did as well as listening to what they said.

  • Behaviour: task success rate, time on task, and whether people searched or navigated.
  • Attitude: System Usability Scale, think-aloud sessions and expectancy tests.
  • What we watched for: whether icons were discoverable, whether labels made sense, whether real routes matched the designed ones, whether every action could be undone, and whether feedback was clear at every step.

Outcome

80%System Usability Scale score
4 + 1native apps and a responsive web app launched

The product launched on Android, on iOS and as a progressive web app. A few principles held throughout and are why it worked for its audience:

  • Lead with visuals: text paired with familiar symbols, real photos and illustrations.
  • Multilingual and hyperlocal, and in line with WCAG.
  • Fewer clicks, and one goal at a time.
  • Personalised, conversational guidance that helps people make informed decisions.
  • The same patterns on app and web, so learning one teaches the other.
  • 3D modelling and VR previews built into the journey, not bolted on.
  • Hooks that build a habit, so people come back between milestones.

Workshops, research & user testing

For Utec, we tested ideas with paper, sticky notes and illustrated cards before writing code, and with analytics after launch. This is the toolkit, and what each method changed in the product.

Client
UltraTech Cement, Aditya Birla Group
Methods
Design thinking workshops, paper prototyping, affinity mapping
Research
Concept testing with cue cards, analytics review
Shaped
Onboarding, home screen and search strategy

Paper first

In workshops, teams sketched screens on paper and walked through them together. Three ideas kept coming back: detect the plot location first, ask what stage the builder is at (I have a plot, I need a plan, I need a contractor, I need materials), and keep a “Call us” option on every screen for people who would rather talk.

Paper is cheap enough to throw away and real enough to argue about. That is exactly what you want at this stage.

Mapping the journey

We laid the whole journey out on large sheets, stage by stage: identifying a need, setting requirements, recruiting stakeholders, building and tracking progress, finishing, and life after finishing. For each stage the group added sticky notes on pains, gains, feelings and expectations.

Some notes were practical: budget overruns, time management, quality of materials. Others were emotional. Before building, people feared being cheated by contractors. Afterwards they felt pride, wanted to give house tours and dreaded criticism. Both kinds shaped what we built.

Designing onboarding

The workshop decisions became flows. Each one removes a reason to give up:

  • Pick a language before anything else. English is selected by default, with Indian languages one tap away.
  • The phone number is the unique ID, auto-detected where possible and verified by OTP. Email and social logins are alternatives.
  • A countdown timer on the OTP screen stops people requesting several codes at once.
  • Only a name (for personalisation) and an email (for recovery) are asked up front.
  • House details are optional. A checklist of activities already done replaces a long Q&A and curates the home feed.

Search vs navigation

After launch, the analytics made a clear case.

Out of 50 modules in the house builder app, three were used far more than the rest:

80.3%used Support
79.9%used the Progress Tracker
79%used Internal Search

Six of the app’s fourteen solutions (42.85%) were search-based: find experts, find materials, the plans gallery, the styles gallery, quick reads and videos. The wider evidence pointed the same way:

59% of web visitors frequently use a site’s internal search, 15% prefer it to the menu, and more than half of people landing on a start page go straight to the search box.Source: Nielsen Group and other behavioural research, as cited in the presentation

Building a house starts with planning, and planning leads people to search for what the market offers. So I recommended putting tab-based search, covering experts, materials, plans, styles, quick reads and videos, at the top of the home screen.

Search is also an internal trigger, the start of a hook loop. Once looking things up in Utec becomes a habit, people start using its other features and putting in their time, data and effort. That raises customer lifetime value.

Home screen options

We explored four directions for the home screen, all built around the same order: identify your stage, browse the design library, turn a sketch into a 2D plan and a 2D plan into 3D, try VR in a UBS store, then budgeting tools and articles. The variations tested colour, density and how strongly each block should call for attention.

Concept cue cards

For formative research, we drew each problem as a scene instead of showing screens. Every card shows a familiar moment of doubt in the builder’s own words: “Will it fit my budget?”, “I wish I had hired a reliable contractor!”, “Will I get a home loan?”

Participants reacted to the problem rather than to our interface, which told us which concepts mattered before we spent effort designing them.
